The Brief

Acre Type's founders came to me with a half-finished idea and a manifesto. They wanted to make exactly one typeface family per year, refuse to discount, and never run a sale. Could I make that idea visible?

The studio had a working font (a humanist sans they'd been refining for two years) and a domain name. They didn't have an identity, a website, a sales channel, or — critically — a way of explaining their pace to people used to the firehose of contemporary type marketplaces.

The work split naturally into three phases: identity and visual system; the marketing and specimen site; and the slow-form materials that would carry the foundry through its first three releases.

A wordmark that argues with itself

The wordmark sets the foundry name in their own typeface, but at two different optical sizes — a display cut for the "ACRE" and a text cut for "TYPE" — joined by a single horizontal rule. It's a quiet argument that this foundry cares about the difference between a letter at 96 points and a letter at 11. Most don't.

Specimen as object

Most foundry websites are catalogs first, specimens second. Acre's reverses that: each typeface gets a long, scrolling, single-page specimen — an essay set in the typeface, with annotations and waterfalls woven into the text. The catalog (such as it is) sits at the bottom, almost as a footnote.

This was the contentious decision. It's slower to navigate. It rewards the patient visitor and quietly punishes the impatient one. It's also the thing that matched the foundry's voice. After three months of waterproofing the argument — A/B tests, conversations, gentle disagreements — we shipped it. The specimens have the highest dwell time of any pages on the site. They also sell.

"I expected a marketing site. They built a magazine that happens to sell typefaces. I'm not even sure how to describe what they made — I just know it convinced me."

— Jordan Tate, founder & type designer, Acre Type

A pace that holds

Fourteen months in, the foundry has shipped two families on schedule, retained 86% of paid customers across releases, and grown its mailing list by an average of 240 readers per month. The marketing budget is zero. The voice has held.

The argument I keep coming back to with this work is that pace is itself a design decision. Acre slowed down. Then they got better. The site, the specimens, and the wordmark are all in service of that one quiet idea.
